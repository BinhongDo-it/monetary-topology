# B139: Japan 1859, one conversion written by holder, and the ring the open ports joined

A treaty made foreign silver pass "same kind, same weight" against the ichibu, while the ichibu kept
passing against the koban at its face of a quarter ryo. The price of one coin in another then differed
edge by edge, and the ring dollar -> ichibu -> koban -> gold sold abroad -> dollar did not close. This
document states the ring, the carriers and the readings. Script `experiments/b139_japan_1859.py`; record
`results/b139_japan_1859.json`.

## 1. The ring

`s_d` fine silver in a dollar, `s_i` fine silver in an ichibu, `g_k` fine gold in a koban (one ryo = four
bu), `R_w` the world silver-gold ratio by weight, `e` the ichibu a rule gives for one dollar:

    L(e) = e * (1/4) * g_k * R_w / s_d

    by weight (the treaty)     e = s_d / s_i          L_w = R_w / R_t,  R_t = 4 s_i / g_k
    by bullion (at home)       e = 4 s_d / (g_k R_b)  L_b = R_w / R_b
                                                      L_w / L_b = R_b / R_t

`R_t` is the silver-gold ratio the ichibu carries at face; `R_b` the ratio on the domestic bullion market.
"Japan's gold-silver ratio" is therefore two numbers, one per edge. A scalar price field, one price per
coin for every holder, sums every ring to zero: `ln L = 0`.

## 2. The floor

Every coin has at least two published specifications: the Mint's 1874 table of old coins, Dillon's 1874
Osaka assays, Koga's assays of 1889-1904, the legal and the assayed Mexican dollar. They differ by 1% to
2.5%, a hundred times the printed digits. All are carried, and the spread of `ln L` across them is the
floor: 0.005 to 0.012 per segment, almost all of it from the Ansei koban, which Koga did not assay.

## 3. Readings

**B139-1** (instrument): on 5,000 synthetic specifications a lot of silver walked round the ring coin by
coin agrees with the three closed forms to `6.4e-16`; four seeded faults (gross weight for fine, ten bu to
the ryo, the world ratio inverted, the bullion edge at face) are caught.

**B139-2** (known answer): the chain in the literature, 4 dollars -> 12 ichibu -> 3 koban -> about 12
dollars, returns 2.97 to 2.99 with the Tenpo koban at four ichibu, three to the printed digit. The Tenpo
koban had been raised to 1 ryo 1 bu on 1859-06-24, a week before the ports opened; the ring open from
1859-07-21 returns 2.38 to 2.40, which the 1866 tariff convention's "a hundred dollars for 311 ichibu"
matches.

**B139-3** (rival, grid B): `ln L` by segment, minimum and maximum over every source combination.

| from | rule | ln L | floor |

| 1859-07-01 | custom house: 1 dollar = 2 two-shu = 1 bu | −0.233 to −0.222 | 0.012 |
| 1859-07-21 | 1 dollar = 3 ichibu | +0.865 to +0.877 | 0.012 |
| 1860-02-22 | koban revalued: Tenpo 3 ryo 1 bu 2 shu, Ansei 2 ryo 2 bu 3 shu | −0.121 to −0.105 | 0.016 |
| 1860-05-30 | Man'en koban at 1 ryo | −0.112 to −0.101 | 0.011 |
| 1860-07-01 | official class: by weight less 4% | −0.101 to −0.096 | 0.005 |
| 1860-07-01 | everyone else: market, autumn 1860 point 2 ichibu | −0.517 to −0.507 | 0.011 |

No segment falls inside its floor; the bullion ring `ln L_b` is +0.19 to +0.40 under six readings of
`R_b`. The rival's point prediction, zero on every ring, fails on every segment, and the sign changes
between segments.

**B139-4**: `R_t` 6.0 to 6.9 for the coins of the opening; `R_b` 10.1 to 12.5 (Kaseikoyo 1887, Ikebe 1896,
two readings of the 1859 foreign-affairs papers, and the Osaka gold rate folded with the chogin
fineness). Endo (1931, p.33) holds that the Osaka quote is a money rate of chogin and not a weight; the
two folded values stand on reading it as a weight.

**B139-5**: the edge dollar <-> ichibu carries nine written rules. From 1860-07-01 it carries two at once,
by holder: the official class by weight less 4%, everyone else at the market.

Who could change how much in December 1859 (Bakumatsu gaikoku kankei monjo 30): at Kanagawa 10,000 ichibu a
day for all nations together, divided among them, out of a mint output of 15,000 to 20,000 a day for three
ports; at Nagasaki nine ichibu a day per American, cut to four; at Hakodate five dollars a day per man of a
Russian warship. The Hakodate magistrates report that the town does not take dollars and that foreign
merchants who drew ichibu at the office changed them at a premium for Japanese holding dollars. Harris,
to the Roju on 17 December: same kind, same weight binds the exchange at the port offices, and trade between
merchants lies outside it; on each koban the Japanese seller and the foreign buyer gained four ichibu apiece.

**B139-6, B139-7**: the issuer closed the ring twice. From the silver side, the heavy Ansei two-shu closed
it on the day the ports opened (−0.23); three weeks later the Roju's letter to Harris set three ichibu to
the dollar and it opened to +0.87. From the gold side, revaluing the koban and then the Man'en recoinage
closed it and went past zero, to −0.10.

At market prices on one day in mid-December 1859, the Roju Wakisaka's talk with Harris (Bakumatsu gaikoku
kankei monjo 30, doc. 39, pp.91-95) gives the koban at 8 ichibu in the Kanagawa market and the dollar at
2 bu to 2 bu 2 shu at the money changers, against 3 ichibu at the custom house. With the world side taken
from the specifications, the ring for a holder with a custom-house quota stands at +0.15 to +0.40 (Ansei
or Tenpo koban), and for everyone else at −0.26 to +0.22, across zero; the written rates of that day open
it at +0.87. The market had narrowed the ring for both, and left it open for the class with the quota.

The gold leg moved at market prices before the issuer rewrote it. The Kanagawa koban stood at 2 ryo by 13
December 1859, +0.47 in logs over the written 1 ryo 1 bu. The oban bought for the treasury in Osaka cost 30
ryo 2 bu on 30 October 1859 and 55 ryo 2 bu on 31 January 1860, +0.60 between the two. The revaluation in
force on 22 February wrote +0.99 for the Tenpo koban: the market had covered 60% of that distance on the oban
before the revaluation was announced.

**B139-10**: the Osaka gold quote day by day, 706 trading days of 1859 and 1860 (the Mitsui ledger). From
30 June 1859 the quote is for gold on hand, the koban and ichibu-ban no longer quoted, so the series after
that day is the gold unit of account in silver of account and the koban ring cannot be folded from it.
What it shows:

- At the decrees on the dollar <-> ichibu edge the quote does not move: over ten trading days before and
  after, +0.004, −0.000, 0.000, −0.004, −0.001 in logs for the five decrees of 1859 and January 1860, against
  a median ten-day step of 0.005 for the whole series; −0.018 on 1 July 1860, near its 90th percentile
  (0.019). Those decrees moved the ring from −0.23 to +0.87. Windows of five and twenty days read the same.
- The day the quote left the koban it fell from 76.42 to 69.65 momme per ryo, −0.093.
- After the revaluation of the old koban it rose to 78.9, and in the month the Man'en koban was issued it
  fell to 69.0, −0.133 within the month; from then it stood near 71, where it had stood in mid-1859.
- Two tenders in the same ledger put four ichibu of silver at the day's quote for a ryo of gold on hand to
  within 0.1% (bids 71.36 to 71.40 against 71.42; 71.400 to 71.435 against 71.450): the ichibu passed at its
  face against gold, with half to two thirds of that face in silver.

The account rate between gold and silver ended where it began, while the koban's written value was moved by
a factor of 2.7 and its gold cut to a third: the adjustment was carried by the class values of the coins.

**B139-8** (bookkeeping): 42 named estimates or statements of the gold outflow, each with its source,
not combined; they span about two thousand times.

**B139-9**: after the edge was cut, 1601 to 1859, `ln(R_w / R_b)`.

- Official rates: 1609, 50 momme: +0.21; 1700, 60 momme: +0.05.
- Sanka zui, each row with the coins it names: +0.06 to +0.22 in the seventeenth century, wide
  intervals through the Genroku and Hoei recoinages, +0.30 to +0.64 under Kyoho, +0.18 to +0.23 in the
  1740s and 1750s.
- From 1763 the gap narrows, to −0.005..+0.10 in 1772, and opens again to +0.25 in 1781 and +0.38 in 1786.
  1763 to 1779 are the years Chinese ships carried gold and silver into Nagasaki, the cut edge running the
  other way. The two-shu silver coin of 1772 is a change on the silver side in the same years and is not
  separated here.
- Osaka daily quotes, annual mean: +0.15 to +0.30 on the Genbun coins (1787-1817); the Bunsei recoinage
  moves the gap to +0.36 in 1820 and the Tenpo recoinage to +0.55 in 1838; it narrows to +0.29 by the first
  half of 1859.
- Unfolded points: Newton's 1717 Mint report (China and Japan, 9 to 10), Ikebe 1855, Kaseikoyo 1859, the
  foreign-affairs papers 1859: +0.20 to +0.52.

**B139-11, B139-12, B139-13**: dated statements, July 1859 to December 1861.
`data/b139_dated_statements.json` carries 278 statements of what a dollar fetched in ichibu, of the koban's
price and of the amounts the custom houses changed, one row per statement with its source and the way its
figure was read: 235 on page images, 12 from a firm's books, 15 in transcription only, and 16 entered as
studies. The kind of source is a column of its own: 22 statements come from later compilations or modern
studies, six of them with figures read on the page image, and none of the 22 enters a reading. No day-by-day market series exists for these years.
Until about 1862 nobody at Yokohama dealt between dollars and ichibu, the exchange being done by the ten
principal money changers of Edo (the Tokyo Higher Commercial School's inquiry of 1914); both series in the
literature begin in 1862; and the Shanghai weekly that reported Japan, 131 issues from July 1859 to December
1861, has no Yokohama market column. The rate the Yokohama custom house posted in July and August 1860 was
a notice that stood until the rate changed. The statements are therefore read by month, in ichibu per 100
dollars, and a month is sorted when one side's whole range lies above the other's. Two rules say which
months count, and both are read. Under the first, a rule in force, everyone else has a statement dated to
within two months and the custom house's side is the written rate that covers the month, however long it
stayed in force. Under the second each side has a statement dated to within two months. A cell is claimed
only where the two rules give the same verdict.

- **B139-11** (rival, grid B): the rate the custom house gave the holders it changed for, against what
  everyone else got. With the rule in force, Yokohama: the custom house above in 8 of 12 months, the two
  ranges overlapping in 4, below in none (sign test `3.9e-03`; by quarter, 7 of 7); Nagasaki: 15 of 16, 1
  overlapping, none below (`3.1e-05`; by quarter, 8 of 8); 23 of 28 at the two ports. With both sides dated:
  5 of 5 at Yokohama, 1 of 1 at Nagasaki. Where a month is sorted the gap is +0.02 to +0.45 in logs. Each
  overlapping month at Yokohama is one in which a measure wrote one value for every holder: shops ordered
  to take the dollar at three ichibu (August 1859), the three-bu stamp (January 1860), the mint buying
  dollars at about three (May and June 1860). The government's own counter gave foreigners +0.41 to +0.44
  more than it gave Japanese holders in the two months where both are dated. One price for every holder
  leaves a month no reason to sort either way; the criterion reads that price as refuted when one side
  holds more than half of all the dated months, and as standing when neither side does.
- **B139-12** (rival, not read): the rate at which the custom house took dollars paid in for duties, 311 by
  weight under the treaty, against what everyone else got. With the rule in force, Yokohama: above in 17 of
  20 months, no sign in 3, below in none (`7.6e-06`); Nagasaki: 3 of 3. The three months without a sign are
  January 1860, when the street's upper figure touched 311, and July and August 1860, when the custom house
  asked for duties at the market rate; that was withdrawn within six weeks and the three ports were ordered
  to take dollars by weight. With both sides dated only August and September 1860 at Yokohama count, one
  without a sign and one above, which the criterion's rule reads as the one price standing. The two rules
  give different verdicts, so no cell is claimed; the months under the rule in force are printed as a
  reading. In the summer of 1860 the one custom house wrote four values for the dollar: the monthly
  allowance of the legations by weight, duties at 311, the posted notice at 233 to 240, and 250 to Japanese
  holders at the mint.
- **B139-13**: the ring at each month's prices, by holder. Dollars to ichibu to koban at Yokohama to dollars
  at Shanghai, September to December 1859. For a holder changing at the custom house the whole range lies
  above zero in all four months (+0.208..+0.685, +0.187..+0.717, +0.059..+0.679, +0.157..+0.411); for a holder
  at the others' rate it sits astride zero in the two months where that rate is dated (−0.197..+0.169,
  −0.248..+0.331). Where the coin is named, 5 to 7 November 1859: +0.429..+0.506 on the Ansei koban,
  +0.344..+0.417 on the Tenpo koban. Six lots and shipments returned 1.26 to 1.65 times their cost, and two
  observers put the multiple at two. The Shanghai leg, valued with gold at a booked sovereign rate: the
  koban sold there for its gold plus its silver at the rates of the dollar, −0.001..+0.030 and
  +0.008..+0.012. The sovereign rate is the one a church's account, dated 31 December 1859, booked 300
  sovereigns at, 3.10 taels each; it is 54 days from the koban's price and the day of the conversion is not
  stated, and no market quotation of the sovereign is on these pages. The dollar's rate in taels near 7
  November is likewise no cash quotation of the day: the month's highest quotation (0.785), a sum booked in
  an account on the 16th (0.7452) and the discount on bank bills payable in Hongkong on the 4th (0.73); the
  record prints each and the ranges without the bills and with the quotation alone, and the flag of this
  criterion is the same without the bills. A koban carried in and paid at its new value under the rule in
  force from 22 February 1860, set against its Shanghai price of November 1859, the one on these pages:
  +0.09 to +0.17. The two are 107 days apart and no Shanghai price after the revaluation is on them, so this
  is the old Shanghai price against the new rule; coin was in fact carried back from Shanghai to pay duties
  on 28 May 1860.

At the custom-house rate demand had no ceiling and what bound was the quota: 30 to 50 dollars a head a day
in August 1859, applications for 1.2e21 dollars on 2 to 4 November 1859, 10,000 ichibu a day for the whole
port from December, nothing for merchants from July 1860. Nine statements describe holders of the written
rate passing it on at a premium. Thirteen seeded faults move the three readings, among them the two classes
exchanged, a later compilation counted (also one whose figure was read on its page image), no dated
statement on the custom house's side, and every holder handed the custom-house rate; if one were not
caught, all three criteria would be recorded as unread.

## 4. Carriers

The Mint's 1874 table (Dajokan notice of 5 September 1874); Dillon 1874; Koga's assay record; Eckfeldt
and Du Bois 1842 and the US Mint reports for the dollar; the treaty of 1858 in English, Dutch and
Japanese; the shogunate's notices of 1859-1860 from the Dai Nihon Ishin Shiryo Kohon images; Bakumatsu gaikoku
kankei monjo 30 (1960) for the talk of Ansei 6.11.20; Soetbeer
1879 and MeasuringWorth for `R_w`; the Mitsui Osaka daily gold rate (Kinsei Keizai Database, read locally
under its terms); Sanka zui, prices part; the mint procedures of about 1790 and Taya 1973 for the coin
specifications; McMaster 1960 and 41 other sources for the outflow. The dated statements of 1859 to 1861
(`data/b139_dated_statements.json`) come from the British and American official papers, Dai Nihon
komonjo: Bakumatsu gaikoku kankei monjo 23 to 51, the Dai Nihon Ishin Shiryo Kohon, the North-China
Herald (all 131 issues of July 1859 to December 1861), The Nagasaki Shipping List and Advertiser of 1861,
Dutch, French and German accounts, merchants' books through McMaster, and the Tokyo Higher Commercial
School's inquiry of 1914; `data/SOURCES.md` lists them group by group.
